# How Model Growth, Recursion, and Boundary Operators Influence Scaling Exponents

**Authors:** Zixi Chen, Akshay Vegesna, Samip Dahal, Andrew Gordon Wilson  
**Date:** 2026-09-16; arXiv v2 2026-09-17  
**Source:** arXiv:2609.19107v2

## Abstract Summary

The paper studies whether architecture can change the exponent of a compute–loss scaling law rather than only its constant. Its common experimental family divides a Transformer into a prelude, a repeated core, and a coda. The authors vary three axes: a boundary operator between stages/passes, weight sharing across repeated core passes, and growth of the number of passes during training. They report that model growth and the boundary operator improve fitted compute scaling exponents over their measured regime, while tied recurrence provides parameter-efficient executed depth. [1]

## Key Findings

1. **Boundary operator:** a matched vanilla-versus-Operator-1 comparison isolates a boundary operation that normalizes the residual stream and reinjects the prelude representation. The authors report a compute multiplier over vanilla that reaches about 1.25x at 10^20 FLOPs. [1]
2. **Model growth:** increasing core passes during training produces the largest reported exponent changes. Untied growth reaches a reported 1.55x compute multiplier over vanilla at 10^20 FLOPs; tied Loop-Grow reaches 1.36x while retaining the stored-parameter advantage of weight sharing. [1]
3. **Data-constrained training:** on a fixed 100M-token pool trained for ten epochs, the optimal loop count rises with compute and loop scaling reaches the best loss of a weight-decay-tuned standard Transformer with a reported 2.2x less compute. [1]
4. **Held-out extrapolation point:** a 7.4B growth model trained at roughly eight times the largest compute used for the fitted ladder lands near the predicted loss curve and has a reported CORE score comparable to the cited GPT-3 13B reference at much lower historical training compute. The authors explicitly note differing data/tokenizer/evaluation context elsewhere in the paper; this is not a controlled head-to-head modern baseline. [1]
5. **Effective depth:** the paper interprets the results through computational depth—the number of layers that meaningfully affect the predictive distribution—rather than nominal stored depth alone. [1]

## Methodology

The architecture is written as a prelude–core–coda system. A prelude produces an early representation `e`; a core is executed `K` times; a coda maps the final state to predictions. Executed depth therefore grows with `K`, while tied looping can reuse the same stored core parameters. [1]

The principal boundary operator is `Norm(h) + αe`: normalize the current residual state and add back the prelude output. The paper uses matched architectural comparisons to separate the effects of the boundary operator, weight tying, and growth. [1]

For single-epoch scaling, each architecture receives an architecture-specific compute-optimal recipe rather than inheriting vanilla hyperparameters. The paper fits compute–loss curves of the form `L(C)=E+A(C/C0)^(-γ)` and reports compute multipliers relative to vanilla. [1]

The paper also runs a repeated-data regime with 100M unique FineWeb tokens over ten epochs, varying loop count and comparing loop scaling against parameter scaling with weight-decay controls. [1]

## Conclusions

The evidence supports a narrower conclusion than the accompanying "towards 10^7 layers" framing: within the studied Transformer family and compute range, boundary normalization/reinjection and staged increases in executed depth can improve measured compute efficiency, and tied recurrence is especially interesting when parameter storage or repeated-data overfitting is constrained. [1]

The strongest scaling claims remain empirical and architecture/recipe dependent. The paper itself surveys mixed prior results for looping and notes that recurrence can perform differently on reasoning and memorization tasks. Extrapolated advantages beyond the measured ladder should therefore be treated as hypotheses for reproduction rather than established universal laws. [1]

## Relevance to current research

For **domain-scaling-lab**, the recurrent core is a concrete empirical system for testing existing work on recurrence cocycles, composed gain, normalization geometry, contraction, spectral modes, and shared active subspaces. The boundary operator also gives a clean ablation target for separating radial normalization from early-state reinjection.

For **long-haul**, tied recurrence suggests a hardware-facing hypothesis: a memory-limited vessel may trade additional time/energy for executed depth without storing proportionally more parameters. This requires a real runnable recurrent artifact and charged comparisons against conventional models and external agent iteration; repeated prompting is not equivalent to latent recurrence.
